By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  1. Construct binomial series for functions of the form \((1+x)^p\).
  2. Use known Taylor series to construct series for other functions.
  3. Apply algebra, substitution, differentiation, and integration to power series.
  4. Use power series to find solutions to differential equations.
  5. Use Taylor series to approximate definite integrals that cannot be evaluated using elementary antiderivatives.
  6. Estimate the error in a series approximation.

Review: Taylor and Maclaurin Series

Recall that the Taylor series for \(f(x)\) centered at \(a\) is

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n } \]

A Maclaurin series is a Taylor series centered at \(a=0\):

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}x^n } \]

When the Taylor series converges to the function, we can use it to represent and approximate that function.

Review: Common Maclaurin Series

Exponential:

\[ e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!} \]

Sine:

\[ \sin x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!} \]

Cosine:

\[ \cos x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{(2n)!} \]

These representations are valid for all real \(x\).

Review: More Maclaurin Series

Geometric:

\[ \frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n, \qquad |x|<1 \]

Natural logarithm:

\[ \ln(1+x)= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n}, \qquad -1<x\leq 1 \]

Inverse tangent:

\[ \arctan x= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{2n+1}, \qquad -1\leq x\leq 1 \]

Question: How can we use these series to represent other functions?

The Binomial Theorem

Recall the binomial expansion for a nonnegative integer \(m\):

\[ (1+x)^m = \sum_{n=0}^{m}\binom{m}{n}x^n. \]

The binomial coefficients are

\[ \binom{m}{n} = \frac{m!}{n!(m-n)!}. \]

For example,

\[ (1+x)^3=1+3x+3x^2+x^3. \]

Question: What happens if the exponent is not a nonnegative integer?

Generalized Binomial Coefficients

For any real number \(p\), define

\[ \binom{p}{0}=1 \]

and, for \(n\geq 1\),

\[ \boxed{ \binom{p}{n} = \frac{p(p-1)(p-2)\cdots(p-n+1)}{n!} } \]

For example,

\[ \binom{p}{1}=p \]

\[ \binom{p}{2}=\frac{p(p-1)}{2} \]

\[ \binom{p}{3}=\frac{p(p-1)(p-2)}{6}. \]

These coefficients allow us to extend the binomial theorem.

Definition: The Binomial Series

For any real number \(p\),

\[ \boxed{ (1+x)^p= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{p}{n}x^n } \]

for \(|x|<1\).

Expanding:

\[ \begin{aligned} (1+x)^p={}&1+px\\ &+\frac{p(p-1)}{2!}x^2\\ &+\frac{p(p-1)(p-2)}{3!}x^3+\cdots \end{aligned} \]

For \(p\) not a nonnegative integer, the radius of convergence is \(R=1\).

Convergence at the endpoints depends on \(p\).

  3. Compute the first four binomial coefficients.
  4. Write the first four nonzero terms.
  5. Express the result using sigma notation.

Constructing New Taylor Series

Suppose

\[ f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_nx^n. \]

We can replace \(x\) by another expression, such as \(bx^m\):

\[ \boxed{ f(bx^m)= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_nb^nx^{mn} } \]

Important:

The resulting series is valid whenever the substituted expression lies in the interval where the original series represents \(f\).

Example 4: Substitution into a Known Series

  3. Simplify the general term.
  4. Determine the radius of convergence.

Differentiating Known Taylor Series

Recall that

\[ f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_nx^n \]

can be differentiated term by term inside its radius of convergence:

\[ \boxed{ f'(x)= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nc_nx^{n-1} } \]

The differentiated series has the same radius of convergence.

Question:

When would differentiation be easier than computing Taylor coefficients directly?

Example 6: Differentiating a Known Series

Use the geometric series

\[ \frac{1}{1-x}= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n \]

to find a power series representation for

\[ f(x)=\frac{1}{(1-x)^3}. \]

Strategy:

  1. Differentiate the geometric series.
  2. Differentiate a second time.
  3. Adjust the constant factor.
  4. Determine the interval of convergence.

Integrating Known Taylor Series

Recall that a power series can be integrated term by term:

\[ \boxed{ \int\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_nx^n\,dx = C+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{c_nx^{n+1}}{n+1} } \]

This is valid inside the original radius of convergence.

Why is this useful?

Some functions do not have antiderivatives that can be expressed using elementary functions.

Power series give us another way to work with these integrals.

Solving Differential Equations with Power Series

Consider a differential equation involving an unknown function \(y(x)\).

Suppose its solution can be represented by

\[ \boxed{ y(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nx^n } \]

Then

\[ y'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}na_nx^{n-1} \]

and

\[ y''(x)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}n(n-1)a_nx^{n-2}. \]

We can substitute these series into the differential equation and determine the coefficients.

Strategy: Power Series Solutions

To solve a differential equation using a power series:

  1. Assume a solution of the form

    \[ y(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nx^n. \]

  2. Differentiate the series as needed.

  3. Substitute into the differential equation.

  4. Rewrite using matching powers of \(x\).

  5. Equate coefficients and solve for the \(a_n\).

  6. Use initial conditions to determine unknown coefficients.

Evaluating Nonelementary Integrals

Some functions have antiderivatives that cannot be expressed in terms of elementary functions.

For example,

\[ \int e^{-x^2}\,dx. \]

Our usual integration techniques do not produce an elementary antiderivative.

Alternative approach:

  1. Represent the integrand as a power series.
  2. Integrate term by term.
  3. Use a finite number of terms to approximate a definite integral.

Example 10: Integrating a Taylor Series

Find a power series representation for

\[ \int e^{-x^2}\,dx. \]

Strategy:

  1. Start with

    \[ e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}. \]

  2. Replace \(x\) with \(-x^2\).

  3. Integrate the resulting series term by term.

  4. Write the answer using sigma notation.

Example 11: Approximating a Definite Integral

Use a Maclaurin series to approximate

\[ \int_0^{1/2}e^{-x^2}\,dx. \]

Questions:

  1. What is the Maclaurin series for \(e^{-x^2}\)?
  2. What series results from integrating term by term?
  3. What approximation do the first four terms give?
  4. How can we bound the error using the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem?

Error in a Series Approximation

Suppose

\[ S=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^nb_n \]

is an alternating series with \(b_n>0\), decreasing to zero.

If we approximate using the partial sum

\[ S_N=\sum_{n=0}^{N}(-1)^nb_n, \]

then

\[ \boxed{ |S-S_N|\leq b_{N+1} } \]

The error is at most the magnitude of the first omitted term.

Important: We must verify the conditions of the Alternating Series Estimation Theorem.

Example 12: Accuracy of an Integral Approximation

\[ 10^{-5}. \]

Questions:

  3. How many terms guarantee the required accuracy?

Application: Probability

The standard normal density function is

\[ \phi(x)= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}. \]

For a standard normal random variable \(Z\),

\[ P(a\leq Z\leq b) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_a^b e^{-x^2/2}\,dx. \]

Because the antiderivative is nonelementary, we can use a power series to approximate this probability.

Summary: Working with Taylor Series

We can use known Taylor series to construct new representations through:

  1. The binomial series
  2. Substitution and algebra
  3. Term-by-term differentiation
  4. Term-by-term integration

We can also use power series to:

  • Find solutions to differential equations.
  • Approximate nonelementary integrals.
  • Estimate approximation errors.

Power series extend the techniques available to us beyond elementary algebra and integration.
