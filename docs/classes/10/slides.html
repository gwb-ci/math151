Moments and Center of Mass

Moments and Center of Mass

Class Session Goals:

  • Find the center of mass of objects distributed along a line.
  • Locate the center of mass of a thin plate.
  • Use symmetry to help locate the centroid of a thin plate.

What is a Moment?

A moment measures how mass is distributed relative to a point.

  • Mass farther from the origin has a greater effect than mass closer to the origin.
  • The direction matters: mass on opposite sides of the origin contributes moments with opposite signs.
  • For a point mass \(m\) located at \(x\), its moment about the origin is \(mx\).
  • For several masses, the total moment is the sum of their individual moments.

The center of mass is the point where the system would balance.

Why Do Moments Matter in Physics?

A moment measures how strongly a mass or force tends to produce rotation about a point.

The farther the mass or force is from the axis of rotation, the greater its rotational effect.

Both distance and mass affect the moment:

\(\text{moment} = \text{mass} \times \text{distance from the axis}\)

Moments translate the geometry of where a mass is into the physics of how strongly it influences rotational motion.

Discrete Moments

Let \(m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n\) be point masses placed on a number line at points \(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\), respectively.

The moment of a system with respect to the origin is \(M=\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i x_i\)

The total mass of the system is \(m=\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\).

The center of mass is \(\bar{x}=\frac{M}{m}\).

The center of mass is the point where the total mass could be concentrated without changing the moment. It is a sort of average that takes into consideration both the location and amount of mass.

Center of Mass on a Line Example

Suppose four point masses are placed on a number line:

\(m_1=30\) kg at \(x_1=-2\) m

\(m_2=5\) kg at \(x_2=3\) m

\(m_3=10\) kg at \(x_3=6\) m

\(m_4=15\) kg at \(x_4=-3\) m

Find the moment of the system and the center of mass.

\(\bar{x}=\frac{M}{m}=\frac{-30}{60}=-\frac{1}{2}\)

Center of Mass on a Plane Formula

Let \(m_1,m_2,\ldots,m_n\) be point masses located in the \(xy\)-plane at points \((x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\ldots,(x_n,y_n)\).

The moments are

\(M_x=\sum_{i=1}^n m_iy_i\)

and

\(M_y=\sum_{i=1}^n m_ix_i\)

The center of mass is

\(\bar{x}=\frac{M_y}{m}\) and \(\bar{y}=\frac{M_x}{m}\)

Center of Mass on a Plane Example

Suppose three point masses are placed in the \(xy\)-plane:

\(m_1=2\) kg at \((-1,3)\)

\(m_2=6\) kg at \((1,1)\)

\(m_3=4\) kg at \((2,-2)\)

The total mass is

\(m=2+6+4=12\) kg

\(\bar{x}=\frac{M_y}{m}=\frac{12}{12}=1\)

\(\bar{y}=\frac{M_x}{m}=\frac{4}{12}=\frac{1}{3}\)

Why Can We Replace a 2D Piece with a Point Mass?

For balance, the shape is not critical.

What is important is the total mass of the piece, and how that mass is distributed relative to each axis

The center of mass is the single point where the piece has the same balancing effect as the whole region.

For moments, a region with mass can be replaced by a point mass located at its center of mass.

Continuous Moments

Suppose a lamina is bounded above by \(f(x)\), below by the \(x\)-axis, and on the left and right by \(x=a\) and \(x=b\).

Let \(\rho\) denote the density of the associated lamina with respect to area.

The mass of the lamina is

\(m=\rho\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\)

Moment with respect to x axis

A representative rectangle has height \(f(x_i^*)\).

Its center of mass is

\((x_i^*,f(x_i^*)/2)\)

The moment with respect to the \(x\)-axis is

\(M_x=\rho\int_a^b \frac{[f(x)]^2}{2}\,dx\)

Moment with respect to y axis

The moment with respect to the \(y\)-axis is

\(M_y=\rho\int_a^b xf(x)\,dx\)

The coordinates of the center of mass are

\(\bar{x}=\frac{M_y}{m}\) and \(\bar{y}=\frac{M_x}{m}\)

Example 1 Mass

Let \(R\) be the region bounded above by \(f(x)=\sqrt{x}\) and below by the \(x\)-axis over \([0,4]\).

Find the centroid of the region.

Assume \(\rho=1\).

\(m=\int_0^4 \sqrt{x}\,dx=\left.\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}\right|_0^4=\frac{16}{3}\)

Example 1 Moments

Compute the moments:

\(M_x=\int_0^4 \frac{x}{2}\,dx\)

\(M_y=\int_0^4 x\sqrt{x}\,dx\)

\(\bar{x}=\frac{64/5}{16/3}=\frac{12}{5}\)

\(\bar{y}=\frac{4}{16/3}=\frac{3}{4}\)

The centroid is \((12/5,3/4)\).

Moments between two functions

For a region between \(y=f(x)\) and \(y=g(x)\), each thin slice has height \(f(x)-g(x)\).

Its mass is represented by its area:

\(\text{Area}=(f(x^*_i)-g(x^*_i))\Delta x\)

Center of mass of rectangle \(y\)-axis: \((x^*_i, \frac{1}{2}(f(x^*_i)+g(x^*_i)))\)

Moments between two functions forumla

The moment about the \(y\)-axis uses the slice’s horizontal position:

\(M_y=\int_a^b x(f(x)-g(x))\,dx\)

The moment about the \(x\)-axis uses the slice’s midpoint height:

\(M_x=\frac{1}{2}\int_a^b \left([f(x)]^2-[g(x)]^2\right)\,dx\)

Example 2

Let \(R\) be the region bounded by \(f(x)=6-x^2\) and \(g(x)=3-2x\). What is the centroid?

First find the intersection points:

\(6-x^2=3-2x\)

\(x^2-2x-3=0\)

\((x-3)(x+1)=0\)

So the region lies on \([-1,3]\). \(f(0)=6\) and \(g(0)=3\). So \(f\) is the upper bound and \(g\) is the lower bound.

Example 2

\(m=\int_{-1}^{3}\left[(6-x^2)-(3-2x)\right]\,dx\)

\(M_y=\int_{-1}^{3}x\left[(6-x^2)-(3-2x)\right]\,dx\)

\(M_x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{3}\left[(6-x^2)^2-(3-2x)^2\right]\,dx\)

\(\bar{x}=\frac{M_y}{m}=1\), \(\bar{y}=\frac{M_x}{m}=\frac{13}{5}\)

The centroid is \((1,\frac{13}{5})\).

Symmetry Principle

Symmetry lets us recognize when opposite parts of a region balance each other.

If a region is symmetric about a line, then the center of mass must lie on that line. We can use symmetry to find one coordinate immediately.

Example 3

What is the center of mass of the region under \(f(x)=2x-x^2\) is bounded by the \(x\)-axis from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\).

Since \(f(x)=2x-x^2\) is symmetric about the vertical line \(x=1\), the region has the same shape and mass on both sides of \(x=1\).

So the center of mass must lie on this line:

\(\bar{x}=1\)

Symmetry lets us find \(\bar{x}\) without computing any moments.

Example 3 x-Moment

The area of the region is

$m=_0^2 (2x-x^2),dx

The moment about the \(x\)-axis is

\(M_x=\frac{1}{2}\int_0^2 (2x-x^2)^2\,dx\)

\(\bar{y}=\frac{M_x}{m}=\frac{8/15}{4/3}=\frac{2}{5}\)

So the centroid of the region is \((1,\frac{2}{5})\).

Example 4

Let \(R\) be the region bounded above by \(f(x)=4-x^2\) \(g(x)=x^2\) below and to the right of the y-axis.

The region is symmetric with respect to the line y=2.

Therefore, the \(y\)-coordinate of the centroid is 2.

\(m=\int_{-2}^{2}(4-x^2)\,dx=\frac{32}{3}\)

Example 4

The moment about the \(y\)-axis is

\(M_y=\int_0^{\sqrt{2}} x\left[(4-x^2)-x^2\right]\,dx=2\)

The mass of the region is

\(m=\int_0^{\sqrt{2}}\left[(4-x^2)-x^2\right]\,dx\)

\(\bar{x}=\frac{M_y}{m}=\frac{2}{8\sqrt{2}/3}=\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{8}\)

The centroid is \(\left(\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{8},2\right)\).
